The valuation gap compares the model's predicted market price with rents capitalised at an assumed user cost. A positive value means prices are above what rents justify under the baseline assumptions; see the sensitivity band below. It is a model-dependent hypothesis, not investment advice.

Relative mispricing is the median residual of the price model: positive = dearer than comparable dwellings elsewhere in the city, negative = cheaper. It says nothing about a bubble.

Two cross-checks of sale prices

Depends on the premium
Check 1, rent side, 2025: is the rent growth the price requires plausible?
Sale prices require rents to grow by 3.4% a year (2.4% to 5.4% for a risk premium of 1% to 4%). Over the last 20 years: rents 4.6% a year and incomes 4.7% a year.
Depends on the rate
Check 2, income side, 2025: can a local household finance the price?
The median sale price is 0.97 times what the reference household can finance (0.86 to 1.09 with the rate one point lower or higher; rate used 4.0%).

Check 1 solves the rent-capitalisation formula for the growth of rents instead of the value; incomes are the ceiling, since rents cannot outgrow them for ever. Check 2 ignores rents: it applies the lending rules (55% of income (total debt servicing ratio), 30 years, 25% down payment, at the regulatory rate floor) to the median household income from work of the planning area. Read together for 2025: mixed: at least one check depends on an assumption. Both checks move with interest rates, and check 2 describes a median local household, not the people who actually buy. They are reported next to the verdict and are not part of its rule. Model-dependent hypotheses, not investment advice.

Prices and ratios through past cycles

Official price index Price-to-income ratio Price-to-rent ratio
  1. 1981 Q2 · Pension savings opened to private purchases, then the 1985 recession (tax or lending rule)
    Peak after the 1981 scheme that let buyers use their Central Provident Fund savings for private homes; the fall lasted until 1986 and spans Singapore's first recession (1985), marked by a construction slump.
    Sources: Stacked Homes, past price falls (secondary source); SG101, The 1985 Recession.
  2. 1996 Q2 · Anti-speculation package, then the Asian financial crisis (financial or economic crisis)
    Prices had more than doubled between 1990 and 1996. The government announced anti-speculation measures on 14 May 1996; the Asian financial crisis from mid-1997 deepened the fall, and the index bottomed in 1998 Q4, 45% below its peak.
    Sources: Chan, Ng and Ramchand, BIS Papers No 64 (2012); Singapore Government press release, 18 Nov 1997.
  3. 2000 Q2 · Dot-com bust and 2001 recession (financial or economic crisis)
    The decline from 2000 Q3 coincides with the end of the dot-com boom and was prolonged by the 2001 recession, the SARS outbreak and the second Gulf War.
    Sources: NUS IREUS study as reported in 2022 (secondary source).
  4. 2008 Q1 · Global financial crisis (financial or economic crisis)
    The index lost about a quarter of its value, then rebounded quickly in 2009 as interest rates fell.
    Sources: Chan, Ng and Ramchand, BIS Papers No 64 (2012).
  5. 2013 Q3 · Cooling measures on stamp duty and lending (tax or lending rule)
    Peak after successive rounds of measures: higher additional buyer's stamp duty in January 2013 and a cap on total debt service introduced in June 2013. Prices then slid for about four years.
    Sources: Stacked Homes, cooling measures since 2009 (secondary source); PropNex, 23 Oct 2015 (secondary source).
  6. 2021 Q1 · Price-to-rent peak; no cause verified (no cause verified)
    Highest price-to-rent ratio of the series. In the following quarters the official rent index rose faster than the price index, which brought the ratio back down. No source read attributes this to one cause.

Numbers mark turning points of the series; the text gives what the sources say happened around each date. These are coincidences in time documented by the sources listed, not causal estimates of this project, and several explanations can hold at once. Ratios are divided by their own average over the period shown, so 100 means "usual for this market", not "fair value".

Market tension and liquidity

Rents are a constrained expense: households must be housed, so a rent-based value can "justify" high prices while the market is stuck. When prices are too high, sellers rarely cut them at once; sales volumes fall first. This section tracks that adjustment. Time on market would be the ideal measure, but it needs listing data, which are not open.
