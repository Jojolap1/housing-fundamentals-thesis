Methodology

1. Research question and stance

ML and hedonic models fitted to transaction prices estimate market value: what a dwelling with given characteristics sells for, given current market conditions. They cannot say whether those conditions are justified. Fundamental value is anchored on rents capitalised at the user cost of housing (Poterba 1984; Himmelberg, Mayer & Sinai 2005, "HMS"). Whether prices exceed fundamentals is a hypothesis tested under explicit, varied assumptions. It is never a premise.

4. Cleaning rules

Every step is counted in data/processed/cleaning_log_<city>.csv, and the identifiers of removed rows are kept in data/interim/removed_rows_<city>.csv.gz (not published).

Paris (DVF): 1. exact duplicate lines; 2. nature_mutation == "Vente" only; 3. mutations made of exactly one apartment, with or without outbuildings (dépendances); 4. price > 0 and 9 ≤ built area ≤ 400 m²; 5. point inside a Paris quartier; 6. non-market prices: price/m² more than 3× above or below the arrondissement × year median. This catches €1 symbolic transfers and similar, which are over 1% of some arrondissement-years, so a percentile trim alone misses them; 7. 1st–99th percentile of price/m² within arrondissement × year.

Floor area is surface_reelle_bati, which is always filled. Carrez area is filled for only about half of the sales and is kept as a robustness variable.

Singapore (HDB resale): 1. price > 0, 25 ≤ area ≤ 300 m²; 2. block geocoded by OneMap; 3. inside an MP2019 subzone; 4. factor-3 non-market rule within town × flat type × year; 5. 1st–99th percentile.

Exact duplicate lines are kept and flagged (dup_flag), because two flats in the same block, month and storey band can sell at the same price.

Geocoding. Paris: geo-DVF coordinates, which are cadastral-parcel centroids (no geocoder needed). Singapore: OneMap search on "block + street". A result is accepted only if OneMap returns the same block number and the same street after HDB abbreviations are expanded ("BT" → "BUKIT", "AVE" → "AVENUE", …). The hit rate is written to data/processed/geocoding_singapore.csv.

5. Ratios

Computed per year at city, district_l1, district_l2 and street level (src/ratios/levels.py):

  • Price/m²: median, IQR (q25–q75) and n. YoY is the change of the median against the same unit one year earlier. The window change is 2022 → 2025 for Paris (the only full years inside DVF's 5-year window) and 2020 → 2025 for Singapore.
  • Rent:
  • Paris: unfurnished reference rent (€/m²/month) of the cell quartier × rooms (1, 2, 3, 4+) × construction period × vintage. Vintages apply from 1 July to 30 June. The construction period comes from the nearest residential BD TOPO building within 30 m of the parcel point. If no building matches, the four periods are averaged, weighted by the quartier's dwelling mix; this is also reported as a robustness variant.
  • Singapore: median monthly rent of HDB approvals for the same flat type and year. It is matched at block level if that cell has n ≥ 5, then subzone, then planning area. rent_source records the level used.
  • Gross yield = 12 × monthly rent / price, per transaction, then the median. Price-to-rent is its inverse, also per transaction, then the median.
  • Price-to-income:
  • Paris: price of a 60 m² dwelling at the transaction's price/m², divided by the annual median disposable income per consumption unit of the transaction's IRIS (Filosofi 2021) (pti_uc). It is also divided by that income × 1.5 UC, a couple without children on the modified OECD scale (pti_hh).
  • Singapore: 4-room HDB resale price / (12 × the planning area's median monthly household income from work). The median is interpolated linearly inside the income band from the Census 2020 band counts; it is undefined if it falls in the open S$20,000+ band.
  • Publication rule: a cell (street, hexagon or district) × year is published only if n ≥ 5.

6. Models

  • Target: log price/m² (sales) and log monthly rent (Singapore HDB rentals).
  • Features: log floor area, rooms or flat type, flat model, construction period, building year and number of floors (Paris), storey mid-point and remaining lease (Singapore), outbuildings, log distance to the nearest metro/RER or MRT/LRT exit, log distance to the CBD anchor, log distance to the nearest public green space ≥ 1 ha, and time.
  • OLS: district_l2 and year fixed effects, HC1 robust standard errors, and also standard errors clustered by district. Moran's I on the residuals uses 10-nearest-neighbour weights on a seeded random sample of 20,000 sales, with 199 permutations.
  • Random Forest (200 trees, min leaf 5, 40% of features per split, 50% bootstrap) and LightGBM (1,500 rounds, learning rate 0.03, 63 leaves). Both see latitude/longitude and a numeric month index.
  • Validation:
  • (a) spatial block CV: H3 resolution-8 cells (about 0.7 km²) are the groups, 5 folds, GroupKFold;
  • (b) time split: train on all years before the latest one, test on the latest. The unseen year's OLS effect is set to the last training year's.

Errors are reported on price/m², after Duan smearing: RMSE, MAE and MAPE. - Interpretability: SHAP (TreeExplainer) summary and dependence plots for LightGBM, compared with the OLS elasticities.

7. Fundamental value and valuation gap

u   = r_f + τ + δ + γ − g
V*  = R / u                                   Paris (freehold: perpetuity)
V*  = R / u · [1 − ((1+g)/(1+i))^T], i = u+g  Singapore (HDB lease, T years left)
gap = P̂ / V* − 1
  • R:
  • Paris: annual reference rent × floor area.
  • Singapore: annual rent predicted by the HDB rent model for the sold flat's type and location. Rents exist only from 2021, so earlier years use the 2021 level and are flagged.
  • P̂: the out-of-fold LightGBM prediction from the spatial CV, so no dwelling is valued by a model trained on its own sale.
  • r_f: mean 10-year government yield of the year. France: ECB series IRS.M.FR.L.L40.CI.0000.EUR.N.Z. Singapore: SingStat M700071, series 15.
  • Assumptions (baseline [sensitivity range]; sources in src/ratios/params.py):
Term Paris Singapore
τ, property tax 0.25% [0.1–0.5%] 0.10% [0–0.3%]
δ, maintenance + depreciation 1.5% [1.0–2.5%] 1.5% [1.0–2.5%]
γ, risk premium 2% [1–4%] (HMS) 2% [1–4%] (HMS)
g, expected growth 2% [0–4%] 2% [0–4%]
  • Sensitivity grid: r_f ± 0, 0.5 and 1 percentage point; γ ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4}%; g ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}%. Combinations with u < 0.5% are reported as undefined, never clipped.
  • Relative mispricing = log(actual price) − log(P̂), aggregated by street, hexagon or district. It measures cheap or expensive relative to comparable dwellings. It is not a bubble indicator.

8. Second pillar: market tension and liquidity

Rents are a constrained expense: households must be housed, so rents can stay high when they are already too heavy, and a rent-capitalisation value then "justifies" high prices while the market is stuck. When prices are too high, sellers rarely cut them at once (loss aversion, Genesove & Mayer 2001); the adjustment shows first in volumes and time on market (Stein 1995). Time on market needs listing data, which are not open for Paris or Singapore, so the pillar uses open data only (src/ratios/liquidity.py):

9. What each tool can and cannot say

Tool Answers Does not answer
Hedonic / ML model on sale prices (§6) What does a dwelling like this sell for today? Which streets are dear or cheap relative to comparable dwellings? Whether the market as a whole is too dear: a model fitted to prices reproduces any general overvaluation
Rent capitalisation at the user cost (§7) Is the price consistent with current rents under stated r_f, γ and g? Whether rents themselves are sustainable (rent is a necessity, and it is regulated in both cities); the answer changes sign inside the plausible range of r_f − g
Affordability ratios (§5, §8) How many years of income, what share of income? A "fair" level, without a history or a benchmark
Price-to-rent dynamics (§11) Is the ratio far from its own history, and do prices tend to correct? The level of fundamental value
Explosive-root tests (§11) Did the series behave explosively, and when? Why; and nothing below city level

No single tool is treated as decisive. §12 combines them under a rule fixed in advance.

10. Composition and small samples

A raw average by neighbourhood moves with which dwellings happen to sell and, in small cells, with a single sale: 100 sales at €1,000/m² and one at €2,000/m² give a mean of €1,010 but a median of €1,000. Three devices deal with this.

Cell statistics (src/ratios/robust.py). For every cell and year: n, median, mean, 10% trimmed mean, IQR, and a 95% percentile-bootstrap confidence interval of the median (1,000 resamples, fixed seed). Maps use the median.

Thresholds. Street and hexagon cells need n ≥ 5 and are otherwise not published at all (DVF reuse terms). District and neighbourhood cells need n ≥ 20 for a full set of statistics; with 5 ≤ n < 20 they are published "greyed", with the count only.

Shrinkage. The log median θ_i of a small cell is pulled towards the log median μ_p of its parent (street → arrondissement or planning area; quartier or subzone → arrondissement or planning area; these → city):

shrunk_i = μ_p + w_i (θ_i − μ_p),   w_i = τ²_p / (τ²_p + v_i)

where v_i is the bootstrap variance of θ_i and τ²_p the dispersion of true cell medians inside the parent, estimated by a precision-weighted method of moments. A cell with few sales has a large v_i and moves towards its parent; a cell with many sales keeps its value. Raw and shrunk values are both published, with the weight w_i. This is a standard empirical-Bayes (random-effects) estimator; no specific paper is cited for it here because the reference has not yet been checked.

Quality-adjusted indices (src/indices/), quarterly, first quarter = 100:

Index Method Controls for Weakness
Hedonic time-dummy (Hill 2013; Eurostat 2013) log price/m² on quarter dummies and characteristics; pooled, and rolling 5-quarter windows chained on the last change observed characteristics (size, rooms or flat type, location, building period, storey, remaining lease) unobserved quality (condition, view, energy class)
Stratified median (Prasad & Richards 2008) fixed-weight geometric mean of strata medians; strata = arrondissement × rooms (Paris), HDB town × flat type, planning area (private) the mix of strata sold mix changes inside a stratum
Repeat-sales (Bailey, Muth & Nourse 1963; Case & Shiller 1989) price changes of dwellings sold twice; Case-Shiller weights everything constant within a dwelling uses only resold dwellings; needs a dwelling identifier
Volume number of retained sales per quarter – a liquidity measure, not a price

Dwelling identifier: in Paris, cadastral parcel + lot number. HDB and URA publish no unit number, so the Singapore series is a pseudo repeat-sales index on a proxy unit (block × flat type × storey band × floor area; project × floor area × storey band) and is labelled as such. Each index is compared with the official index of the market (Notaires-INSEE for Paris apartments, HDB resale price index, URA private residential price index) on the common quarters: correlation of quarterly changes and gap in cumulative change (index_validation_<city>.csv). Differences are reported, not adjusted away.

11. Dynamics: price-to-rent and explosive roots

Price-to-rent (src/valuation/price_rent.py). Following Gallin (2008), and the present-value logic of Campbell & Shiller (1988), k-quarter changes in log prices and log rents are regressed on the lagged log rent-price ratio (k = 16). A positive coefficient in the price equation means prices correct towards rents. Newey-West standard errors with k−1 lags are reported; Gallin shows that such regressions are biased in small samples and uses a bootstrap, so the t-values are indicative. The z-score of the latest log price-to-rent ratio in its own history is also reported.

Explosive roots (src/valuation/explosive.py). SADF and GSADF statistics and the backward sup ADF (BSADF) date-stamping of Phillips, Shi & Yu (2015), applied to housing as in Pavlidis et al. (2016). Settings: series in logs; Dickey-Fuller regression with a constant and no lagged differences; minimum window 0.01 + 1.8/√T of the sample; critical values from 999 simulated random walks with drift 1/T and a fixed seed; an episode needs BSADF above its 95% critical value for at least log(T) quarters.

  • Advantage: no level of fundamental value is needed, so the test does not inherit the weaknesses of the rent anchor.
  • Limits: it needs a long, regular series, so it runs on city-level official indices (Notaires-INSEE from 1991, URA from 1975, HDB resale price index), never on streets; an explosive price can reflect explosive fundamentals, so the test is also run on the price-to-rent ratio where a rent index exists (Singapore private, from 2004); indices are nominal; results depend at the margin on the minimum window and the lag order.

12. Verdict rule

Fixed before the results (src/valuation/verdict.py). Each anchor gives +1 (prices above fundamentals), 0, −1 or "not run":

Anchor Rent-based +1 when
User cost yes the gap is positive in at least 80% of the defined sensitivity-grid cells of the latest full year (−1 when in at most 20%)
Price-to-rent yes the latest log ratio is more than one standard deviation above its historical mean
Explosive root no GSADF rejects at 5% and a dated episode includes the latest quarter
Affordability no price-to-income more than one standard deviation above its history (not run without a history)
  • Evidence of deviation: at least three anchors at +1, one of them not rent-based.
  • Consistent with fundamentals: at least two anchors run, none at +1, user cost at −1.
  • Inconclusive: everything else.

The thresholds (80%, one standard deviation) are conventions. verdict.csv lists, for each market, the signals, the anchors that agree and those that could not be run, with the reason. Liquidity (§8) is reported next to the verdict; it is not an anchor.

Limitations

These are stated plainly because they bound what the results can say.

Rents

  • Paris rents are regulatory reference values, not contracts. They are set by prefectoral order from observed rents (OLAP). The "reference" is roughly a median, and the legal ceiling is +20% above it. A gap computed with them compares prices with regulated-market rents. Observed market rents (and especially furnished lets) can be higher, which would lower the measured gap.
  • The rented and the owned stocks differ. Reference rents describe the private rental stock, while DVF describes the dwellings that were sold. Matching on quartier × rooms × construction period reduces, but does not remove, the composition difference.
  • Construction period is not in DVF. It comes from the nearest residential BD TOPO building within 30 m of the parcel centroid (matched for about 86% of sales). A wrong building match puts a sale in the wrong rent cell. The period-mix rent is the robustness check.
  • Singapore rents are HDB approvals to rent out whole flats, with the declared rent and no floor area. They cover 2021 onwards only: for 2017–2020 the rent model is held at its 2021 level, so gaps for those years are weaker evidence. Room rentals are excluded.

Prices

  • DVF covers only the last five years (legal rule), currently July 2021 – June 2026, so there is no Paris history before the 2022 rate shock, and 2021 and 2026 are half-years.
  • DVF has no floor level, condition, view or energy class. Hedonic residuals therefore mix unobserved quality with genuine mispricing.
  • The DVF price covers the whole mutation, including outbuildings (parking, cellar); outbuildings are a model feature.
  • HDB resale prices are recorded by month only. Storey is a 3-floor band.
  • Exact duplicate HDB lines are kept: they cannot be told apart from distinct sales.
  • Singapore private housing (URA) covers non-landed apartments and condominiums only, from November 2021 (URA serves the last 5 years). Rental areas are 10 m² bands, so rent/m² is approximate. Transaction locations are the project's, not the unit's.
  • URA terms of use not yet verified: publication of aggregates derived from the URA API depends on them.
  • HDB public housing is a regulated market: eligibility rules, CPF financing and grants all shape HDB prices.

Income

  • Paris: median disposable income per consumption unit, by IRIS, for 2021 only. The household version assumes a 1.5-UC couple.
  • Singapore: household income from work only, in bands, from the 2020 Census, by planning area. Households with no employed member (13% of the total) are excluded, and several small planning areas are pooled into "Others" and get no value.
  • The two price-to-income definitions are not strictly comparable across cities.

Fundamental value

  • Only r_f is measured. τ, δ, γ and g are assumptions. The sensitivity grid shows how far the conclusion moves with them, and it moves a lot, as it does in HMS (2005). A gap is evidence conditional on those assumptions.
  • The user-cost model is a steady-state arbitrage condition. It ignores credit constraints, transaction costs, taxes on rental income, and (for Paris) rent regulation itself, which caps R.
  • For HDB, the finite lease is handled by discounting over the remaining lease. Policy around lease expiry (e.g. VERS/SERS redevelopment) is not modelled.

Models and spatial statistics

  • Spatial block CV uses H3 resolution-8 cells. Larger blocks would be stricter.
  • OLS residuals remain spatially autocorrelated: Moran's I = 0.06 for Paris, 0.25 for Singapore sales, 0.04 for Singapore rents. The tree models, which see coordinates, capture more of this.
  • The HDB rent model has no floor area or storey (not in the source): its MAPE is about 15%, against 5-7% for HDB prices.
  • HDB demand is restricted (eligibility, 5-year minimum occupation before renting out, grants, CPF rules), so the arbitrage behind rent capitalisation is constrained. A negative HDB gap is not evidence that flats are available below their value to any buyer.
  • Moran's I is computed on a 20,000-sale sample with k = 10 neighbours.
  • Distances are straight-line, not walking distances. The CBD anchor is a single point.

Data access

  • The OSM Overpass API and Geofabrik were unreachable from the build environment, so stations and parks come from official sources instead (IDFM, Ville de Paris, LTA, NParks). This is an improvement in provenance, but OSM-based features are not reproduced.

Second pillar (tension and liquidity)

  • Time on market and sale-to-list discounts, the most direct measures of a stuck market, need listing data that are not open; turnover and volume-price divergence are proxies.
  • Paris turnover uses the 2023 census stock for all years; DVF 2021 and 2026 are half-years (annualised for turnover, excluded from year-on-year changes).
  • HDB stock: 16,236 flats (1.5%) are in blocks whose HDB town could not be inferred from the resale file and are left out of the denominators.
  • URA serves developer sales for about the last three years only (from November 2023).
  • Paris incomes are held at their 2021 level (Filosofi), so the Paris rent-to-income trend is overstated by income growth since 2021.

Robust statistics, indices and tests (added 2026-11-10)

  • Status. These modules are tested on synthetic data only (tests/test_indices_valuation.py). Their outputs on the real data have not been produced yet, so nothing here has been compared with an official index so far.
  • Verdicts are inconclusive for now. verdict.csv currently rests on the user-cost anchor alone (share of sensitivity-grid cells with a positive gap in 2025: Paris 86%, Singapore private 87%, HDB 19%). One anchor cannot give a verdict under the rule.
  • Bootstrap intervals treat the sales of a cell as independent draws; sales in the same building or project are not, so intervals are somewhat too narrow. For very small cells the bootstrap distribution of a median is coarse.
  • Shrinkage assumes the cells of a parent are exchangeable. A street that truly differs from its arrondissement and has few sales is pulled towards it: the shrunk map is smoother than reality, which is why raw values stay published.
  • Hedonic indices hold observed characteristics constant, not condition, view or energy class. The pooled version fixes the characteristic prices over the whole period.
  • Repeat-sales. Paris: at most five years of DVF, so few dwellings sell twice and those that do (short holding periods) are not representative. Singapore: no unit identifier, so only a pseudo repeat-sales index on a proxy unit, where two different flats can be paired.
  • Explosive-root tests use nominal official indices, a fixed lag order of 0 and simulated critical values; they date explosive episodes at city level and say nothing about streets or about the level of fundamental value. No long rent series has been found for Paris, so the price-to-rent version runs for Singapore private housing only.
  • Paris window. DVF+ open-data is listed as covering 2014 to 2025 (see the data audit, §10). Until that is tested, all Paris results rest on July 2021 to June 2026.
  • HDB history before 2017 is not ingested, so the HDB index starts in 2017.
