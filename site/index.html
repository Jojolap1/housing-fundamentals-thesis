Are housing prices justified by fundamentals?

Machine-learning and hedonic models trained on transaction prices estimate market value. They cannot, by construction, say whether that market value is justified. This project anchors fundamental value on rents capitalised at the user cost of housing (Poterba 1984; Himmelberg, Mayer & Sinai 2005) and treats overvaluation as a hypothesis to test, not a premise.

Why not value a home the way markets value a company?

Because markets do not know what a company is worth either. They know its price. The value of a share is its future dividends discounted, P = D / (r − g), and the answer hangs on two numbers nobody observes: with a gap of 4 points between the discount rate and growth, one point less raises the value by a third and one point more lowers it by a fifth. Shiller (1981) showed that share prices move far more than later dividends justify. The formula used here for housing, V* = R / (user cost − g), is the same one, with rent in place of the dividend, and it is just as sensitive.

What a stock exchange has, and housing lacks, is a mechanism that keeps the price informative and pulls it back when it strays:

  • No identical asset. One share equals another. Every dwelling is different and sells rarely, so even the price level has to be rebuilt with quality-adjusted indices.
  • No continuous quote. A few thousand sales per quarter, published months later, against millions of trades a day.
  • No short selling. Someone who finds a flat too dear cannot sell it short; they can only stay out. Prices are then set by the most optimistic buyers (Miller 1977), and it is the number of sales that falls first.
  • A cash flow that is hard to see. Companies publish audited accounts every quarter. The "dividend" of a home is a rent, often regulated, or never paid at all when the owner lives in it.

Housing therefore combines the valuation uncertainty of equities with a market that corrects slowly: in the panel of 39 countries used here, a crash in house prices takes a median of 25 quarters from peak to trough. So this project does not look for one fair price. It asks what one must believe for today's prices to be justified, whether that belief is plausible, and it reports the answer as a range checked against independent evidence.

Method in five lines

  1. Official transaction registers are cleaned and harmonised into one schema per city (every removed row is logged).
  2. Hedonic OLS (district fixed effects), Random Forest and LightGBM predict price per m²; they are validated with spatial block cross-validation and a time split.
  3. Annual rent R comes from regulatory reference rents (Paris) or HDB rental approvals (Singapore).
  4. Fundamental value V* = R / (rf + property tax + maintenance + risk premium − expected growth); for 99-year HDB leases, R is discounted over the remaining lease only.
  5. Valuation gap = predicted price / V* − 1, reported with a sensitivity grid over rf, the risk premium and growth.
